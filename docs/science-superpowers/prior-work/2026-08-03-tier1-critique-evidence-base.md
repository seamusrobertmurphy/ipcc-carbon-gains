# Published criticism of IPCC Tier 1 soil methods: evidence base

Compiled 2026-08-03 from three parallel reviews: the reference stocks and Batjes, the
stock-change method, and Latin American national practice. Evidence classes are load-bearing.
PEER = peer-reviewed, DOI verified against Crossref or OpenAlex. GREY = official report, read.
ABSTRACT = metadata and abstract only, full text not opened. SNIPPET = search-result text only.

**All three agents lost their WebSearch budget and worked against Crossref, OpenAlex, Europe
PMC and government file stores directly.** Peer-reviewed nulls below are reliable; grey and
preprint coverage is thinner than usual and the nulls there are weak.

---

## 1. The headline: our critique is unclaimed, and that is checkable

**No published paper evaluates, validates or contests IPCC Table 2.3 / SOCref as such.** One
agent enumerated **all 70 works citing Batjes (2011)**: every one is a *user* of the values,
none an auditor. Searches for anyone arguing the arithmetic mean is the wrong statistic for a
reference stock returned nothing.

**Nobody has tested the multiplicative form** `SOCref x F_LU x F_MG x F_I` against measured
chronosequences, or compared it with an additive or input-driven alternative.

**Nobody has made the reporting-versus-crediting distinction for the wetland or coastal
factors.** It exists in the literature only for agricultural mineral soils.

These three nulls are the paper's openings and should be stated, not left implicit.

## 2. The keystone citation we were missing

**Sanderman, J. & Baldock, J.A. (2010).** *Accounting for soil carbon sequestration in national
inventories: a soil scientist's perspective.* **Environmental Research Letters 5:034003. DOI
10.1088/1748-9326/5/3/034003.** PEER, open access, full text read by the agent.

A sustained attack on our exact estimator, quoted in our own notation. Verbatim:

> "the use of response ratios is inconsistent with the current scientific understanding of
> carbon cycling in soils and response ratios will overestimate the net-net sequestration of
> soil carbon if the baseline is not at steady state."

> "If, as most researchers believe, SOC sequestration is primarily driven by enhanced C inputs
> to the soil system, then **scaling responses by stock size is likely not the most appropriate
> methodology and, in fact, contradicts the concept of carbon saturation in soils.**"

Quantifies the bias at **9, 20 and 33%** for three non-steady-state scenarios. And states our
conclusion, in 2010:

> "the current recommended IPCC accounting methodologies **may not properly credit these
> activities** and may indeed result in **contradictory results when accounted for using tier I
> or II versus tier III approaches**."

**Use as the anchor for the structural critique.** It makes our contribution the extension to
wetlands and the metric dimension, not the observation itself.

## 3. The tropical analogue of the Irish divergence

**Assad, E.D. et al. (2013).** *Changes in soil carbon stocks in Brazil due to land use: paired
site comparisons and a regional pasture soil survey.* **Biogeosciences 10:6141. DOI
10.5194/bg-10-6141-2013.** PEER, open access. 17 paired sites plus >100 pasture soils,
6.58 S to 31.53 S, across Cerrado, Atlantic Forest and Pampa.

The same Brazilian pasture soils give **opposite signs depending on the counterfactual**:

| Counterfactual | Result |
|---|---|
| Paired adjacent native plot | pasture **loses** 11.0 Mg C/ha at 30 cm |
| Mapped native-vegetation reference stock (Bernoux) | pasture **gains** 6.7 Mg C/ha, +15% |

Same country, same biomes, same pool, opposite sign, and the sign turns entirely on whether the
baseline is a neighbouring plot or a mapped reference stock. **This is the tropical analogue of
the Irish both-directions failure and it is the single most useful citation the reviews found
for the paper's thesis.** Assad et al. also report that sand content and mean annual temperature
together explain roughly half the variance in pasture soil carbon, which a climate-zone by
soil-class lookup cannot represent.

## 4. The tropical organic-soil defaults rest on one region, and say so

**Swails, E., Frolking, S., Deng, J. & Hergoualc'h, K. (2024).** *Degradation increases peat
greenhouse gas emissions in undrained tropical peat swamp forests.* **Biogeochemistry
167:59-74. DOI 10.1007/s10533-023-01110-2.** PEER, ABSTRACT. Verbatim:

> "As current default IPCC EF for tropical organic soils are based **only on data collected in
> SEA ombrotrophic peatlands**, expanded geographic representation and refinement of peat GHG EF
> by nutrient status are also needed."

> "current Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) guidelines **do not provide default
> emission factors (EF) for anthropogenically-degraded undrained organic soils.**"

Latin America and Caribbean (LAC) against Southeast Asia (SEA), undegraded to degraded:

| Flux | SEA | LAC |
|---|---|---|
| Peat CO2, Mg CO2-C/ha/yr | −2.9 ± 1.8 → 4.1 ± 2.0 | −4.3 ± 1.8 → 1.4 ± 2.2 |
| Peat CH4, kg C/ha/yr | 22.1 ± 13.6 → 32.7 ± 7.8 | **218.3 ± 54.2 → 165.0 ± 4.5** |
| Net peat GHG, Mg CO2-e/ha/yr | −7.9 ± 6.9 → 20.7 ± 7.4 | **9.8 ± 9.0 → 24.3 ± 8.2** |

**Two findings that go straight into our argument.** LAC intact peat swamp emits roughly **ten
times** the methane of its SEA counterpart, and degradation *reduces* LAC methane while
*raising* SEA methane, so the sign of the methane response to disturbance is opposite between
the two regions a single default is drawn from. And **LAC undegraded peat is already a net GHG
source of 9.8 Mg CO2-e/ha/yr despite being a CO2 sink of 4.3 Mg CO2-C/ha/yr**. That is our
central mechanism, measured, in the tropics, on undrained peat.

LAC undegraded methane converted to mass: 218.3 × 16/12 = **291.1 kg CH4/ha/yr** (agent's
arithmetic, stated so it can be checked). Compare our coastal flip threshold of 193.7.

## 5. The grassland management factors, tested and failing

**Fujisaki, K. et al. (2015).** *From forest to cropland and pasture systems: a critical review
of soil organic carbon stocks changes in Amazonia.* **Global Change Biology. DOI
10.1111/gcb.12906.** PEER, ABSTRACT. 21 studies, 52 sites, Brazil, Colombia, French Guiana and
Suriname. Verbatim:

> "**Management of pastures, whether they were degraded/nominal/improved, had no significant
> effect on SOC response.**"

That is the F_MG classification tested on its own terms across four countries and failing.

**Eze, S., Palmer, S.M. & Chapman, P.J. (2018).** *Soil organic carbon stock in grasslands.*
**Journal of Environmental Management 223:74-84. DOI 10.1016/j.jenvman.2018.06.013.** PEER,
ABSTRACT. Meta-analysis, 341 datasets. Verbatim:

> "an overall significant reduction (**−8.5%**) in the stock of SOC in global managed
> grasslands, mainly attributable to grazing (−15.0%), and only partially attenuated by
> **fertilizer addition (+6.7%)** and liming (+5.8%)... Management activities had the greatest
> effect in the tropics (−22.4%)."

**Fertilisation at +6.7% against an IPCC improved-grassland factor of +14%**, and a net managed
grassland effect that is negative. This is the strongest counter to F_MG = 1.14 and it lands
directly on the nitrogen trap: the fertiliser gain may be half what the factor claims, before
any N2O is charged.

**Braz, S.P. et al. (2013).** SSSAJ 77:914-928, DOI 10.2136/sssaj2012.0269. PEER, ABSTRACT.
Cerrado Brachiaria pastures: the improvement gain scales with clay content from 6-7 Mg C/ha at
11-16% clay to **47 Mg C/ha at 67% clay**, a sevenfold range within one biome. And degraded
pasture came out at or near native levels, inconsistent with a sub-unity degraded factor.

**Conant, R.T., Cerri, C.E.P., Osborne, B.B. & Paustian, K. (2017).** *Grassland management
impacts on soil carbon stocks: a new synthesis.* **Ecological Applications 27:662-668. DOI
10.1002/eap.1473.** PEER, ABSTRACT. The authors of the IPCC's own source, revisiting it:

> "The main area in which the new data are contrary to our previous synthesis is in conversion
> from native vegetation to grassland, where we find that across the studies **the average rate
> of soil carbon stock change is low and not significant.**"

In Latin America essentially all pasture is converted native vegetation, so that is the relevant
transition. **Conant et al. 2017 is not cited anywhere in the 2019 Refinement Chapter 6**, whose
five "Conant" mentions are all 2001 or 2002. Checkable and worth stating.

## 6. The 20-year transition, and a correction to our own record

**IPCC concedes the point in its own text.** 2019 Refinement Vol 4 Ch 2, verbatim:

> "**Although, soil organic C stock changes in response to management changes may often be best
> described by a curvilinear function**, assumption (ii) greatly simplifies the Tier 1
> methodology..."

> "D = ... **Commonly 20 years, but depends on assumptions made in computing the factors FLU,
> FMG and FI.**"

**Poeplau, C. et al. (2011).** GCB 17:2415-2427, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2486.2011.02408.x. PEER,
ABSTRACT. 95 studies, 322 temperate sites. Grassland establishment reached **128 ± 23% at 100
years** and **no new equilibrium within 120 years**, while losses equilibrated fast: grassland to
cropland in 17 years, deforestation in 23.

**This COMPLICATES us and must be stated.** For grassland establishment Tier 1 **truncates** the
gain rather than inflating it. Any naive "Tier 1 overcredits" framing is wrong and a referee
will find it.

**Poulton, P. et al. (2018).** GCB 24:2563-2584, DOI 10.1111/gcb.14066. PEER, full text read.
Rothamsted long-term experiments: arable-to-grassland equilibrium at **about 100 years**, and
"increases are reversed if the modified management practice is not continued indefinitely."

### CORRECTION APPLIED 2026-08-03

Our `CLAUDE.md` claimed `T_on` (Eq 2.23) and `D` (Eq 2.25) are "asymmetric at 20 years for gains
and 1 year for losses". **Verified against the primary source and corrected in commit 51a1b8d.**
`T_on` does carry that asymmetry but its stock term `Cn` is **dead wood and litter**. `D`, the
mineral soil transition, is "commonly 20 years" with **no asymmetry**. Do not build an argument
on a mineral-soil gain/loss asymmetry: it does not exist.

## 7. Prior art for our Monte Carlo, by the factor authors

**Ogle, S.M., Breidt, F.J., Eve, M.D. & Paustian, K. (2003).** GCB 9:1521-1542, DOI
10.1046/j.1365-2486.2003.00683.x. PEER, ABSTRACT. Verbatim:

> "US agricultural soils accrued 1.3 Tg C yr−1... with a 95% confidence interval ranging from a
> loss of 4.4 Tg C yr−1 to a gain of 6.9 Tg C yr−1. **Most of the uncertainty was attributed to
> management factors**... **we are not able to conclude with 95% confidence that change in US
> agricultural land use and management between 1982 and 1997 created a net C sink.**"

Exact structural precedent for our P(benefit > 0) < 0.95 result, on the same estimator, by the
IPCC's own lead author. **Strengthens rather than scoops**: it shows the result type is credible
and that nobody has done it for the wetland pathways.

**A citable tension.** Table 6.2 advertises **±11%** on improved grassland temperate, **±9%**
tropical, **±7%** on F_I high. Ogle et al. attribute *most* of total inventory uncertainty to
those same management factors.

## 8. Mangroves: the error flips sign with geomorphology

**Rovai, A.S. et al. (2018).** *Global controls on carbon storage in mangrove soils.* **Nature
Climate Change 8:534-538. DOI 10.1038/s41558-018-0162-5.** PEER, ABSTRACT. Verbatim:

> "mangrove SOC stocks have been **underestimated by up to 50%** (a difference of roughly
> 200 Mg ha−1) **in carbonate settings** and **overestimated by up to 86%** (around 400 Mg ha−1)
> **in deltaic coastlines**."

Systematic, not random, and it flips sign with coastal geomorphology. Colombia has a Caribbean
carbonate coast and a Pacific terrigenous one; no single national default can be right on both.

**Breithaupt & Steinmuller (2022)**, GRL, DOI 10.1029/2022gl100177, argue explicitly against a
single default: "this common approach of upscaling from a single local-scale rate obscures
critical environmental differences in burial rates." Their revised global mean of **1.386
t C/ha/yr** sits about 14% below the Table 4.12 default of 1.62.

**Kauffman, J.B. et al. (2018)**, Biology Letters 14:20180208, on the Amazon, the largest
mangrove area in the Americas: soil carbon stocks **relatively low at 340 Mg C/ha**, and "low
soil carbon stocks may be related to **coarse textured soils coupled with a high tidal range**",
neither of which the Supplement stratifies on.

## 9. What Latin American compilers actually did

| Country | Soil pool | What they used |
|---|---|---|
| **Brazil** | Included | **Replaced Tier 1 wholesale.** Bernoux et al. (2002) national soil-vegetation map instead of Table 2.3; nationally derived F_LU, F_MG, F_I with a **four-class pasture condition scheme** applied by state. Tier 1 survives only as gap-fill |
| **Guyana** | Included | **Hybrid.** Country-specific stock of **58.7 t C/ha from 118 plots**, combined with **unmodified IPCC Tier 1 factors** and the 2019 Refinement Eq 2.25. Grassland and Wetland reported **NE**, despite acknowledged coastal deep peat |
| **Costa Rica** | **Excluded** | SOC listed as `excluded` with **no justification given anywhere** in the FREL. Mangrove mapped as a land class with no soil pool |
| **Ecuador** | **Not reported** | SOC sampled and under analysis but never reported. `Moretal` (Mauritia palm swamp, the Amazonian peat-forming type) carried as a **biomass stratum with no soil pool**. COI: declare |

Sources are the BUR5 (Brazil), the June 2025 revised REDD+ reference level and 2024 BUR
(Guyana), the January 2025 FREL (Costa Rica) and the 2020 FREL (Ecuador). All GREY, all read.

**The meta-finding, and it is the best framing available for the Irish case.** No Latin American
or Guiana Shield inventory says an IPCC Tier 1 soil default gave an implausible answer. Brazil
substitutes without comment. Guyana substitutes without comment. Costa Rica and Ecuador exclude
the pool. **The critique lives entirely in the peer-reviewed literature; the inventories act on
it silently.** Ireland is unusual not in diverging but in *documenting* the divergence.

## 10. HWSD, which our global areas depend on

**Köchy, M., Hiederer, R. & Freibauer, A. (2015).** *Global distribution of soil organic carbon.*
**SOIL 1:351-365. DOI 10.5194/soil-1-351-2015.** PEER, open access. Verbatim:

> "The Harmonized World Soil Database (HWSD) v.1.2... giving a total mass of **2476 Pg** when
> using the original values for bulk density. Adjusting the HWSD's bulk density of soil high in
> organic carbon results in a mass of **1230 Pg**, and additionally setting the BD of Histosols
> to 0.1 g cm−3... results in a mass of **1062 Pg**. The uncertainty in BD of Histosols alone
> introduces a range of **−56 to +180 Pg C**... **larger than estimates of global soil
> respiration.**"

A factor of **2.3** in the same database from bulk density alone, and the Histosol term alone
exceeds global soil respiration. **This belongs in our Methods**, because our global pathway
areas come from HWSD and our subject is peat. Köchy et al. also report **quantiles rather than
means**, which is the only published precedent for our mean-versus-median argument and should be
cited as precedent rather than overstated.

## 11. Saturation, which undermines a stock-proportional factor

**Moinet, G.Y.K. et al. (2023).** GCB 29:2384-2398, DOI 10.1111/gcb.16570. PEER, ABSTRACT:

> "almost none take SOC saturation into account. Here, we show that **including saturation in
> estimations decreases any potential contribution of SOC sequestration to climate change
> mitigation by 53%-81%** towards 2100."

**Georgiou, K. et al. (2022)**, Nature Communications 13, DOI 10.1038/s41467-022-31540-9, map
mineral-associated carbon capacity from 1,144 profiles: current stocks are "only 42% and 21% of
the mineralogical capacity" in surface and deeper layers. Makes headroom empirical rather than
assumed.

**Stewart, C.E. et al. (2007)**, Biogeochemistry 86:19-31, DOI 10.1007/s10533-007-9140-0, is the
saturation concept paper. Note that **Paustian and Conant are authors on both the IPCC factor
derivation and the saturation critique**.

**van Groenigen, J.W. et al. (2017)**, Environ. Sci. Technol. 51:4738-4739, DOI
10.1021/acs.est.7b01427, *Sequestering Soil Organic Carbon: A Nitrogen Dilemma*: the nitrogen
required to build stable SOC is itself the binding constraint. **Complements our nitrogen trap
by a stoichiometric route rather than an N2O one.**

## 13. Do not repeat without checking

1. **The Atlantic Forest weak-spot claim is NOT documented.** No publication isolates the Mata
   Atlântica and tests the IPCC reference stock against it. Assad et al. include it in the
   sampling frame but do not disaggregate. **If we make the claim we need a source none of the
   three reviews found.**
2. **"Passed through two revisions unchanged"** must be narrowed. Table 6.2 is headed
   "(UPDATED)"; the 2019 Refinement re-derived the **grazing** factor with a new bibliography and
   left improved grassland on its 2006 basis. Correct form: *the improved-grassland factors are
   unchanged while the rest of the table was revised.* The 2006 Ch 6 PDF is missing from
   `04.references/standards/IPCC-pdf/`; retrieve and confirm before asserting.
3. **The Brazil-versus-Table-2.3 comparison rests on an agent's inference** that Brazilian
   Oxisols map to the LAC class. Do the soil-class join properly first.
4. **Mantovani et al. (2023)** "201-396 Mg C/ha to 1 m" is SNIPPET only.
5. **Guerrini et al. (2024)** on the 30 cm minimum being "deficient" is from body text the agent
   did not open; the abstract supports only a recommendation to sample to 1 m.
6. **Segnini et al. (2018)** abstract reads as if even degraded pasture exceeded forest. Needs
   full text to resolve direction.
7. Everything marked ABSTRACT above needs the full text before a specific number is quoted, per
   the project's own rule.
