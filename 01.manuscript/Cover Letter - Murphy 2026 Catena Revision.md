## Reviewer 1

> The manuscript addresses an important question concerning whether reported soil-carbon gains translate into net climate benefits. Combining carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide across management pathways is useful, and the distinction between absolute emissions and benefits relative to continued drainage is particularly relevant. However, several interpretations of the IPCC guidance and methodological choices require correction before the central conclusions can be supported. I recommend major revision.

I thank the reviewer. Each point was checked against its primary source before any change was made.

**Comment 1.1**

> Lines 193-202 and 816-823 state that drained-soil methane and nitrous oxide factors lack published uncertainty intervals. This is incorrect. Tables 2.3, 2.4 and 2.5 provide uncertainty information for land methane, ditch methane and direct nitrous oxide. For example, the shallow-drained temperate grassland nitrous oxide factor is 1.6, with a 95% interval of 0.56-2.7 kg N2O-N ha-1 yr-1.

The Monte Carlo now samples every published uncertainty range, on both the drained and the rewetted side. The headline result held. Section 2.7 lists the sampled terms (lines 245 to 253), and sections 3.1 and 4.5 were revised to match.

**Comment 1.2**

> Section 4.2, particularly lines 743-750, argues that extending the drained-soil fallback to rewetted soils is an assumption introduced by this study. However, Chapter 3 explicitly recommends nutrient-poor factors for boreal sites and nutrient-rich factors for temperate sites when nutrient status is unknown, for both carbon dioxide and methane.

Section 4.2 now presents the nutrient fallback as an IPCC default for both drained and rewetted soils (lines 745 to 748). It cites Chapter 2 for drained soils and Chapter 3 for rewetted soils. No number changed, because the code already applied the rule to both.

**Comment 1.3**

> Lines 53-60, 500-503 and 834-855 imply that greenhouse gases remain uncombined in the reporting framework. UNFCCC reporting already requires aggregate emissions and removals in CO2 equivalents using GWP100, together with uncertainty reporting. Separating calculation methods across chapters is therefore insufficient evidence that the overall framework ignores the combined balance.

The revised text cites Decision 18/CMA.1, the reporting rules of the Paris Agreement. It also states exactly what the analysis tests. National totals use a single 100-year horizon, and no rule sets a pathway's gain against its own methane and nitrous oxide. The recommendation in section 4.3 now points to paragraph 37, which already allows other metrics. The Introduction (lines 33 to 38 and 53 to 61), section 3.7, the Discussion and the Conclusions were revised to match.

**Comment 1.4**

> Section 3.6 acknowledges that an arithmetic mean can be appropriate for estimating an aggregate stock, yet the Discussion and Conclusions repeatedly label its excess over the median as "bias." A mean exceeding the median in 34 of 37 classes is insufficient to demonstrate inflated national carbon gains.

The manuscript now calls the excess of the mean over the median the skew of the source profiles. It no longer treats that skew as evidence that national gains are inflated. The word bias is kept for its meaning in the 2019 Refinement, a default that differs from the land it is applied to. The Irish factors show that directly. The abstract, Introduction, Discussion, Conclusions and sections 3.5, 4.1 and 4.5 were revised to match. Tables S10 and S11 now head that column "Mean above median (%)".

**Comment 1.5**

> Figure 3 Explain why organic-soil pathways described as indefinite are plotted at 100 years and why seagrass receives a numerical duration despite Table S5 reporting no estimate.

Figure 3 now places the pathways without an end date in a panel of their own. No pathway is given a number of years the guidance does not set. Seagrass sits in that panel, and Table 3 reads "No published ceiling" for it. The reviewer's Table S5 is now Table S6.

**Comment 1.6**

> Section 4.3, particularly after lines 763-769. The discussion of coastal wetland emission factors would benefit from considering microbial controls alongside salinity. Please consider citing Ni et al., 2025, Water Research 287, 124357, which links microbial abundance to geographic variation in sediment greenhouse-gas production potential. This evidence could strengthen the discussion of biological variability underlying the regional applicability of default emission factors, while distinguishing incubation-based production potentials from field fluxes.

A new paragraph in section 4.3 considers microbial controls alongside salinity (lines 768 to 796). It cites Ni et al. (2025), as suggested, with Block et al. (2025) and Seyfferth et al. (2020). It separates incubation evidence from field fluxes. It closes on what microbial variability means for a default keyed to salinity.
